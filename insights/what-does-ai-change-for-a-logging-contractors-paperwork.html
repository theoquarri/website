What does AI change for a logging contractor's paperwork?

Theo Leslie · 4 min read

← AI and data by vertical and segment

Two things, and a logger should find out which matters more. The first is time: photographed trip tickets, scale tickets and settlements read into records without keying, and invoices and payroll drawn from them. The second is checking. A logging business is paid from records other people keep, the mill's scale tickets and the buyer's settlement, and AI makes it cheap to match each one against the logger's own record of each load. A missed load, a wrong product rate or a load credited to someone else can show up in a week. No published figure says how often that happens, so the answer for any crew comes from a month of its own loads.

What the usual answer says

The usual answer presents AI as saving time: digital trip tickets, automated load tracking, invoicing and payroll, less time in the office. It treats the paperwork as a chore to shrink. Some of it also decides what the business gets paid.

The state of the business

The margin for error is small. Timber Harvesting's 2026 Logger Survey drew 193 respondents, mostly small, owner-run operations: 61% run a single crew. Loggers who said they broke even or lost money rose "from 22% in 2021 to 29% last year". Some 85% said mill closures in their area had hurt their company in the past year, and 13% were operating under 50% capacity. The magazine's 2024 survey found that 61% "buy at least some of their own timber", which makes the logger a payer as well as a payee.

A 2023 survey of one state's logging firms by Troy Bowman and colleagues, published in Forests and read as its abstract, found the average respondent was 56 and "about 35% of owners expected to exit the industry within five years".

What AI does with the paperwork

It reads what arrives in whatever form: photographed trip tickets, scanned or emailed scale tickets, PDF settlements. It turns each into records and matches them load by load, using the ticket number where there is one and the date, truck and weight where there isn't. It applies the contract or buyer rate by product and lists differences with the evidence: this load, this ticket, this rate. The logger or the office manager decides what to chase.

The same records answer questions a small business struggles with: cost and revenue per job, what a tract actually paid, which mill or buyer pays most reliably. With mill closures moving where wood can go, those answers bear on where to deliver and which jobs to take.

The one-month test

A crew needs its own load record, kept daily: one line per load, with date, tract, truck, product, destination and ticket number if known, plus a photo of each trip ticket. At the end of the month, match every settlement line and scale ticket against it.

Count four kinds of discrepancy: a load in your record with no ticket or settlement line; a load paid at a different product rate than delivered; a ticket carrying another contractor's number; and a deduction not in the contract. Price each one. Then compare the total with what the checking cost, in time or in fees. If a month turns up nothing, or less than the cost of looking, the gain for that crew is time, not checking. If it turns up a pattern, such as one mill that often misses a product code, the checking pays and the pattern is worth raising with that mill.

Keep the month's results. They are the evidence for that conversation. They are also the baseline for the next month, when the checking can run weekly, while each load is still fresh in the driver's memory and the ticket is easy to find.

When checking is unlikely to pay

Many buyers now issue electronic scale tickets and settlements tied to a contractor number. Where those are accurate and tied to the logger's own load numbers, there may be little to find, and the paperwork that costs most time lies elsewhere: insurance, payroll and compliance. Insurance was the challenge loggers cited most in the magazine's recent surveys. Contractors paid by the day, the acre or a fixed sum, with no load-based pay, have less to check too.
