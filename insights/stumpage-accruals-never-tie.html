Why stumpage accruals never tie to the invoice

Theo Leslie · 6 min read

← All insights

When a stumpage accrual doesn't tie to the invoice, almost nobody's arithmetic is wrong. The number moved somewhere between the scale house and the ledger, and the bill of lading is where it went.

Every month the same conversation happens in forestry finance teams. The accrual says one thing, the invoice from the licensing authority says another, and somebody spends three days with two spreadsheets open trying to work out which is right.

It is rarely a calculation error. The volumes are usually fine. What breaks is the join.

The bill of lading is the only real key

A load leaves the block with a bill of lading. That BOL is the one identifier that survives the whole journey: it is on the scale ticket, it is on the mill's receipt, it appears in the authority's invoice backup, and it is what the ledger entry ultimately represents.

Everything else drifts. Ticket numbers get reissued. Dates differ depending on whether you mean the day the truck was loaded, the day it crossed the scale, or the scaling month the authority assigned it to. Species get recorded as a pay species upstream and a Crown species code downstream. Destinations are four-digit codes in one system and mill names in another.

If you are not matching at bill-of-lading level, you are not reconciling, you are comparing two totals and hoping.

Where the gap actually comes from

  • Timing. A load scaled on the 29th can land in the following scaling month. The accrual books it in one period, the invoice in another, and the monthly totals never agree even though every individual load is correct.
  • Rates that moved. Fuel surcharges and service charges are often accrued at a standing rate and invoiced at a current one. We have seen a fuel surcharge accrued at $1.50 a tonne arrive invoiced at $9, a 500% gap that had nothing to do with volume.
  • Loads with no stumpage. Private-land wood, salvage and internal transfers move through the same scale house and look identical on a ticket, but never appear on a Crown invoice.
  • Format. Some authority backup arrives as a spreadsheet. Some arrives as a scanned PDF, rotated, at whatever angle it went through the feeder.

That last one matters more than it should. A reconciliation that works beautifully on the Excel backup falls over entirely when a hundred pages of scanned invoice arrive, and the team quietly goes back to keying them in by hand.

What a working reconciliation looks like

The version that holds up has four properties.

It reads every format

Spreadsheet backup and scanned PDFs both have to land in the same structure, normalised to BOL level, with no re-keying. Scanned pages need reading by column position rather than by reading order, because a rotated invoice destroys token order but preserves geometry.

It freezes what it has already agreed

Accruals should be drafted, reviewed, then frozen to an append-only ledger. Once a row is agreed it does not silently change when someone reruns the report. Corrections arrive as new rows, not edits.

It tolerates small differences and flags large ones

Nobody wants a variance report listing every load that differs by a tenth of a tonne. Set tolerances on both volume and value, flag what exceeds them, pass what doesn't, and age anything that stays unmatched.

It posts the result

A reconciliation that ends in a spreadsheet is half a process. The release should write back: the original accrual marked released, an equal and opposite reversal, and a variance adjustment for the true-up, then out as an input file the finance team can post without retyping anything.

The part people underestimate

Most of the value is not the time saved, though that is real. It is that a reconciliation which actually ties tells you things you could not otherwise see: rates that have drifted, loads that never got invoiced, accruals ageing quietly on the balance sheet, and the occasional credit sitting in your favour that nobody had noticed.

You cannot find any of that by comparing two totals.
